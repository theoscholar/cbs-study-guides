1 · A family broken by loss — Ruth 1:1–5
Heart of it: tragedy strips Naomi of everything, and the chapter begins where courage is hardest — with nothing left.

When famine struck Israel, Naomi’s husband moved the family from Bethlehem to Moab. There her husband died, leaving her with two sons in a foreign country. The sons married Moabite women who did not worship Jehovah. About ten years later, both sons died, neither having fathered children. Naomi had lost her husband, both sons, and any hope of grandchildren.

2 · Two women, one decision — Ruth 1:6–22
Heart of it: loyalty is chosen on the road, when going back would be easier.

Hearing that Jehovah had again given his people food, Naomi set out for Bethlehem, a journey of perhaps a week. Ruth and Orpah went with her, but Naomi selflessly urged them to return to their mothers, not wanting to rob them of remarriage and children. Orpah wept and left. Ruth wept and stayed, vowing that Naomi’s people would be her people and Naomi’s God her God. In Bethlehem, Naomi lamented that Jehovah had left her bitter and empty-handed, yet Ruth simply began gleaning in the fields to support them both.

Ruth 1:16, 17

“But Ruth said: ‘Do not plead with me to abandon you, to turn back from accompanying you; for where you go I will go, and where you spend the night, I will spend the night. Your people will be my people, and your God my God. Where you die I will die, and there I will be buried. May Jehovah do so to me and add to it if anything but death should separate me from you.’”

Ruth 1:20, 21

“She would say to the women: ‘Do not call me Na·oʹmi. Call me Maʹra, for the Almighty has made life very bitter for me. I was full when I went, but Jehovah made me return empty-handed. Why should you call me Na·oʹmi, when it is Jehovah who opposed me and the Almighty who caused me calamity?’”

3 · Gleaning, Boaz, and the threshing floor — Ruth 2–3
Heart of it: quiet, daily faithfulness opens the way to refuge.

A wealthy landowner named Boaz, the son of Rahab, noticed Ruth working hard in the fields. He learned how selflessly she supported Naomi, praised her for taking refuge under Jehovah’s protective care, and made sure the men would not harass her. Naomi, excited, explained God’s provision for younger widows: a close relative of the dead husband could marry the widow so that a son might be born in the husband’s name and the family line and inheritance remain intact (Deut. 25:5, 6). Ruth agreed, though the custom must have seemed strange and intimidating. At the threshing floor she humbly reminded Boaz of God’s law; deeply impressed with her courage and loyal love, he agreed to see whether he could act on her hope.

Dig Deeper 1
How was the Israelite provision of gleaning unique in the ancient Near East? (ia 39 ¶23, ftn.)
Model answer

Under God’s Law, landowners had to leave grain for the poor, the foreigner, and the widow, so gleaning was a right, not a favor. Ruth, a widowed Moabitess, could therefore work in Boaz’s field with dignity and support herself and Naomi.

Advanced answer

Elsewhere, a stranger’s survival depended on a landowner’s goodwill. In Israel, Jehovah’s Law built care for the vulnerable into the harvest itself, and Boaz went beyond the Law, ordering his men to pull out grain for Ruth and to protect her.

Ruth 2:2

“Ruth the Moʹab·i·tess said to Na·oʹmi: ‘Let me go out, please, to the fields and glean among the ears of grain behind whoever looks on me with favor.’ So Na·oʹmi said to her: ‘Go, my daughter.’”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — In Israel, gleaning was not charity handed out when someone felt generous; the Law made it a right of the poor, the foreigner, and the widow.

Dig Deeper 2
Why can we conclude that Ruth did not approach Boaz with impure motives? (ia 47 ¶17-18)
Model answer

Ruth acted openly at Naomi’s direction, following God’s provision for a repurchaser, not in secret for herself. She asked Boaz to spread his skirt over her as repurchaser, and Boaz himself praised her loyal love, noting she had not chased younger men, rich or poor.

Advanced answer

Her conduct was public and lawful: she went to the threshing floor as Naomi instructed, spoke plainly, and left before dawn unrecognized. Boaz called her an excellent woman, known at the city gate, the opposite of a hidden, improper approach.

Ruth 3:10, 11

“At that he said: ‘Blessed may you be of Jehovah, my daughter. You have expressed your loving-kindness better in the last instance than in the first instance, in not going after the young fellows whether lowly or rich. And now, my daughter, do not be afraid. All that you say I shall do for you, for everyone in the gate of my people is aware that you are an excellent woman.’”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — Everything Ruth did was in the open, under Naomi’s direction and under God’s Law — and Boaz himself said so.

Background. The printed reference for this question (ia 47 ¶17-18) was not among the sources available for this guide, so no quotation from it is reproduced here. The answer above rests on Ruth 3:9–11 and on the chapter narrative (chap. 15, p. 75).

Dig Deeper 3
What is significant about Boaz addressing Ruth as “daughter”? (Ruth 2:8; w16.11 3)
Model answer

By calling Ruth daughter, Boaz showed fatherly care and protection, not romantic interest. He kept her close to his young women, ordered the men not to touch her, and gave her water and food, treating a foreign widow as family.

Advanced answer

The word set the tone: guardian, not suitor. It explains why Ruth felt safe, why she could glean without harassment, and why his later praise of her loyal love carried weight. He had watched over her as a father would, not as a man pursuing her.

Ruth 2:8

“Then Boʹaz said to Ruth: ‘Listen, my daughter. Do not go away to glean in another field, and do not go anywhere else; stay close by my young women.’”

Ruth 2:12

“May Jehovah reward you for what you have done, and may there be a perfect wage for you from Jehovah the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to seek refuge.”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — “Daughter” told Ruth, and everyone listening, that Boaz meant to protect her, not pursue her.

Background. The Watchtower reference printed with this question (w16.11 3) was not among the sources available for this guide, so no quotation from it is reproduced here. The answer above rests on Ruth 2:8–12 and on the chapter narrative (chap. 15, pp. 74–75).

Dig Deeper 4
In contrast to the man called So-and-so, how did Boaz show unselfishness when he agreed to marry Ruth? (w23.03 14)
Model answer

So-and-so refused to repurchase because it might ruin his own inheritance. Boaz accepted the full cost, the land and marriage to Ruth, putting Elimelech’s name and Naomi’s family ahead of his own interests.

Advanced answer

So-and-so counted the cost and walked away. Boaz counted it too, then paid it publicly before the elders, acquiring Ruth to restore the dead man’s name. His gain was a family restored; the cost, whatever the marriage meant for him, he accepted willingly.

Ruth 4:6

“To this the repurchaser said: ‘I am unable to repurchase it, for I may ruin my own inheritance. Repurchase it for yourself with my right of repurchase, because I am not able to repurchase it.’”

Ruth 4:9, 10

“Then Boʹaz said to the elders and all the people: ‘You are witnesses today that I am buying from Na·oʹmi all that belonged to E·limʹe·lech and all that belonged to Chilʹi·on and Mahʹlon. I am also acquiring Ruth the Moʹab·i·tess, the wife of Mahʹlon, as a wife to restore the name of the dead man to his inheritance, so that the name of the dead man will not be cut off from among his brothers and from the city gate of his home. You are witnesses today.’”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — So-and-so protected his inheritance; Boaz spent his to restore someone else’s family.

Background. The Watchtower reference printed with this question (w23.03 14) was not among the sources available for this guide, so no quotation from it is reproduced here. The answer above rests on Ruth 4:1–10 and on the chapter narrative (chap. 15, p. 75).

4 · Reward at the city gate — Ruth 4
Heart of it: Jehovah turns empty hands into a full house.

The next day, Boaz went to the elders of Bethlehem and arranged everything properly. The nearer repurchaser declined, Boaz married Ruth, and in time she bore a son. Naomi cherished her precious grandson, Obed. King David descended from that child, as did the Messiah centuries later (Matt. 1:5, 6, 16).

“Jehovah generously rewarded the courage of Naomi and Ruth!”

For discussion
In what ways did Naomi and Ruth show courage?
Model answer

Naomi kept going after losing her husband and both sons, returning to Bethlehem with nothing. Ruth left her home, family, and gods to stay with Naomi, worked hard gleaning to support them both, and later humbly followed Naomi’s counsel at the threshing floor. Both trusted Jehovah while grieving.

Advanced answer

Their courage was quiet and steady, not a single brave moment. Naomi faced an empty-handed return and bitter grief; Ruth faced poverty, foreignness, and widowhood. Each chose loyal love over self-protection, and Jehovah rewarded them with Boaz, Obed, and a place in the line of David and the Messiah.

Ruth 1:16, 17

“But Ruth said: ‘Do not plead with me to abandon you, to turn back from accompanying you; for where you go I will go, and where you spend the night, I will spend the night. Your people will be my people, and your God my God. Where you die I will die, and there I will be buried. May Jehovah do so to me and add to it if anything but death should separate me from you.’”

Ruth 4:17

“Then the neighbor women gave him a name. They said, ‘A son has been born to Na·oʹmi,’ and they named him Oʹbed. He is the father of Jesʹse, David’s father.”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — Naomi kept walking when she had nothing left, and Ruth kept walking beside her. That steady loyalty is what courage looked like for them.

Background. The chapter’s own summary line (chap. 15, p. 74): “Two grieving women had to endure their losses and find refuge among Jehovah’s people.” The Bible account assigned for this chapter is Ruth 1:1–4:22.

5 · Reflect on the lessons — and meditate on the bigger picture
Heart of it: their story is ours to imitate, and Jehovah’s purpose is ours to trust.
Reflect on the Lessons 1
How might Ruth’s example of diligence and gratitude help us when we suffer financial hardship?
Model answer

Ruth did not wait for rescue; she asked to glean, worked from morning to evening, and thanked Boaz for his kindness. In hardship, we can likewise work diligently at what is available, accept help gratefully, and trust Jehovah to provide day by day.

Advanced answer

Her diligence was steady, not frantic, and her gratitude kept bitterness out. Even an ephah of barley was received as provision. That pattern, honest work, thankful acceptance, and trust, steadies us when income shrinks.

Ruth 2:17

“So she continued to glean in the field until evening. When she beat out what she had gleaned, it came to about an eʹphah of barley.”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — Work at what is in front of you, be thankful for what it brings, and let Jehovah handle the rest, one day at a time.

Background. Ruth gleaned through the day until evening (Ruth 2:7, 17, as summarized in the chapter narrative, chap. 15, pp. 74–75) and took the leftover roasted grain home to Naomi (Ruth 2:14, 18).

Reflect on the Lessons 2
When Naomi was brokenhearted, Ruth helped her to cope. What do we learn from Naomi about humbly accepting help from others? What do we learn from Ruth about encouraging others?
Model answer

Naomi, though bitter and empty-handed, let Ruth stay, work, and later guide her toward Boaz. Ruth encouraged not with speeches but with presence, loyal work, and patience. We learn to accept help without pride and to comfort others by staying close and working alongside them.

Advanced answer

Naomi’s humility shows in letting a younger foreign widow provide for her. Ruth’s encouragement shows in constancy: she did not argue Naomi out of grief but shared the load, gleaned daily, and brought home both grain and hope.

Ruth 2:20

“At that Na·oʹmi said to her daughter-in-law: ‘May he be blessed by Jehovah, who has not failed in his loyal love toward the living and the dead.’ Na·oʹmi continued: ‘The man is related to us. He is one of our repurchasers.’”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — Accepting help is humble; giving it is often just staying, working, and not leaving.

Background. Naomi’s bitter words in Bethlehem (Ruth 1:20, 21) did not drive Ruth away; Ruth answered grief with work, and Naomi’s praise of Jehovah in Ruth 2:20 marks the turn from bitterness toward hope.

Reflect on the Lessons 3
In what other ways might you imitate the courage of Naomi and Ruth?
Model answer

We can imitate them by staying loyal when life empties our hands, by choosing Jehovah’s people and ways over comfort, and by doing the next faithful thing, working, helping, and trusting, even while grieving.

Advanced answer

Imitation looks ordinary: returning to the congregation when discouraged, caring for an aging parent, welcoming a foreigner, or speaking up for a widow. Courage, for them, was loyalty practiced daily until Jehovah turned loss into blessing.

Ruth 4:15

“He has restored your life and will sustain you in your old age, because he has been born to your daughter-in-law, who loves you and is better to you than seven sons.”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — Copy their courage in small, daily loyalties, and leave the ending to Jehovah.

Background. The women of Bethlehem spoke Ruth 4:15 to Naomi after Obed’s birth; the chapter closes on the same note (chap. 15, p. 75): “Jehovah generously rewarded the courage of Naomi and Ruth!”

Meditate on the Bigger Picture 1
What does this account teach me about Jehovah?
Model answer

Jehovah notices quiet loyalty. He saw two grieving widows, provided through his Law and through Boaz, and turned their emptiness into fullness, a son, a name, and a line leading to David and the Messiah.

Advanced answer

He is the God who repays loyal love with more than survival: restoration, family, and a part in his purpose. Ruth sought refuge under his wings, and he gave her a home, a husband, and a place in the ancestry of Jesus.

Ruth 2:12

“May Jehovah reward you for what you have done, and may there be a perfect wage for you from Jehovah the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to seek refuge.”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — Jehovah does not overlook quiet loyalty; he repays it, often beyond what we asked.

Background. Boaz’s prayer in Ruth 2:12 became the shape of the whole account: refuge under Jehovah’s wings, then a perfect wage — marriage, a son, and a name in Israel.

Meditate on the Bigger Picture 2
How does this account tie in with Jehovah’s purposes?
Model answer

Through Ruth, a Moabitess, Jehovah kept the line to David and to the Messiah alive. Her loyal love became part of his purpose to bless all families through Abraham’s seed, showing that foreigners who seek refuge under his wings belong.

Advanced answer

The book bridges the judges and the kings: from famine and death to Obed, Jesse, David, and finally Jesus. It shows Jehovah using faithful, ordinary people, not armies or kings, to carry his purpose forward.

Ruth 4:17, 22

“Then the neighbor women gave him a name. They said, ‘A son has been born to Na·oʹmi,’ and they named him Oʹbed. He is the father of Jesʹse, David’s father.” … “Oʹbed became father to Jesʹse; and Jesʹse became father to David.”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — A Moabite widow’s loyalty became a link in the chain to David, and to Jesus.

Background. The chapter notes the outcome explicitly (chap. 15, p. 75): “King David descended from that child, as did the Messiah centuries later! (Matt. 1:5, 6, 16).” Matthew 1:5, 6, 16 is cited, not quoted, in this guide.

Meditate on the Bigger Picture 3
What would I like to ask Naomi and Ruth in the resurrection?
Model answer

I would ask what kept them going on the hardest days, Naomi, when she felt empty-handed, and Ruth, when she left everything familiar. And I would thank Ruth for showing that loyal love can outlast grief.

Advanced answer

I would ask Ruth what she felt when Boaz first called her daughter, and Naomi what it was like to hold Obed after calling herself empty. Their answers would teach me how grief, patience, and trust end in joy.

Ruth 4:16

“Na·oʹmi took the child and held him to her bosom, and she cared for him.”

SAY IT YOUR WAY — The woman who called herself empty ended up holding a grandson. I want to hear how that felt.

Background. This is a personal meditation question; there is no single right answer. The chapter’s closing line (chap. 15, p. 75) gives the frame: “Jehovah generously rewarded the courage of Naomi and Ruth!”
