The flight — Sisera runs for shelter
Heart of it: A defeated general runs for shelter, and the only person at the tents is a woman with a mind of her own.

Sisera fled the plain of Jezreel, where he had left his men to die without their leader. His mighty army, with its 900 fearsome chariots, had been defeated by a poorly armed force of Israelite foot soldiers, and now he thought only of where he could hide. He made his way toward the tent of Heber the Kenite. In general, the Kenites were allies of Israel, their connection going back to the days of Moses, whose wife came from a Kenite family. But Heber differed from other Kenites: instead of being allied with Israel, he was allied with Canaanite King Jabin. So Sisera, as Jabin’s general, assumed he could take refuge in the tents of Heber. It apparently did not occur to Sisera that Heber’s wife, Jael, might have a mind of her own. Jael was at the encampment, while her husband was away. When she saw Sisera approaching, she had to think fast. (p. 82; summary of the chapter’s opening, not a quotation)

The decision — Jael chooses Jehovah’s side
Heart of it: Jael chooses to side with Jehovah, then lulls his enemy to sleep.

Later, Deborah composed an inspired song about Barak’s victory over Sisera. It said: “Most blessed of women is Jael the wife of Heber the Kenite; she is most blessed of women living in tents.” So Jael was not some scheming, vicious woman, as some critics have described her. Rather, she was a brave fighter in the wars of Jehovah. Like Rahab before her, she chose to side with Jehovah and against his enemies. She knew that Sisera was a wicked foe of Jehovah and that it was up to her to do what Jehovah wanted done. The question was, How? Of course, Jael knew well that hand-to-hand combat was not an option. Sisera was a seasoned warrior and surely a strong man. So she invited him into her tent for refreshment and rest. She covered him with a blanket. When he asked her for water, she gave him milk. He ordered her to stand guard in front of the tent and to lie to anyone who might ask whether there was a man inside. Then he fell fast asleep. (pp. 82-83; summary of the chapter’s account, not a quotation)

The deed — and the lesson
Heart of it: One slip would have meant a horrible death; Jael did not slip. We fight a spiritual war now, and we need Jael’s courage more than ever.

Jael knew that her moment had arrived. She picked up tools that she likely used often — a heavy mallet and a tent pin, both probably wooden. Cautiously, she approached the sleeping warrior. Bending down near his head, she prepared to strike. One slip, one false move at this moment, would surely have meant a horrible death for Jael. But Jael did not slip. In a moment, the deed was done and Sisera lay dead at her feet—a tent pin through his temples! In time, Judge Barak came in pursuit of Sisera. Jael went out to meet him. She said, “Come and I will show you the man you are looking for.” Barak entered the tent, and he saw that Deborah’s prophetic words had been fulfilled: “It will be into the hand of a woman that Jehovah will give Sisera.” In their victory song, Barak and Deborah gratefully praised Jael. Thereafter, Jael disappears from the Bible record. Still, down to this day, over 32 centuries later, her story lives on. Today, God’s servants do not strike literal blows with weapons or tools. But we are at war, a spiritual war. And today more than ever, we need the courage of Jael. (p. 83; summary of the chapter’s account, not a quotation)

For discussion · p. 83
In what ways did Jael show courage?
Main Point

Jael acted alone, with no army behind her. When the defeated general Sisera came to her tent, she thought fast, invited him in, and gave him milk and rest. Then, with only a tent pin and a mallet — tools she used every day — she killed a seasoned warrior while he slept, knowing one slip would mean her death.

Additional Point

Her courage was a choice, not a reflex. Like Rahab before her, she sided with Jehovah against his enemies, and she did what she could with what she had — no sword, no soldiers, no rank. The song later called her “most blessed of women,” and Jehovah remembered her deed for over thirty-two centuries.

1. What connection is there between the battle fought by Sisera and the battle of Armageddon? (w19.09 8-9 ¶3-6)
Main Point

Jehovah connected his final war with Megiddo because of what happened there. By the waters of Megiddo, he helped Barak defeat Sisera’s army, and Deborah’s song ended by asking Jehovah to destroy his enemies while saving those who love him. Armageddon follows that same pattern.

Additional Point

There is one difference the Watchtower highlights: at Armageddon, God’s people will not fight and will not even be armed. Their strength will be in keeping calm and showing trust, while Jehovah and his heavenly armies win the victory — by whatever means he chooses.

And they gathered them together to the place that is called in Hebrew Armageddon.

“Why did Jehovah connect the final great war with Megiddo? Megiddo and the nearby Jezreel Valley were the sites of many battles. On occasion, Jehovah directly intervened in those battles. Barak and the prophetess Deborah thanked Jehovah for their miraculous victory. They sang: ‘From heaven the stars fought . . . against Sisera. The torrent of Kishon washed them away.’—Judg. 5:19-21.”

Their ‘strength will be in keeping calm and showing trust’ in Jehovah and his heavenly armies.—Isa. 30:15; Rev. 19:11-15.”

2. Jael’s husband, Heber, allied himself with Israel’s enemies the Canaanites. But how was Heber related to the Israelites? (it “Heber” No. 2)
Main Point

Heber was a Kenite, a descendant of Hobab, whose son-in-law Moses was — so he was related to Israel through Moses’ own family. But he had separated himself from the rest of the Kenites and was at peace with Jabin, the king of Hazor. His wife Jael, though, chose Jehovah’s side.

Additional Point

The Kenites in general were allies of Israel, and their connection went back to Moses, whose wife came from a Kenite family. Heber broke with that loyalty — yet Jehovah still used his household, and his wife, to carry out his judgment on Sisera.

3. How did Israel’s victory over Sisera and Jabin strengthen the faith of God’s people many years later? (w08 10/15 14-15 ¶12-15)
Main Point

Many years later, the psalmist prayed for Jehovah to do to Israel’s enemies what he had done to Sisera and Jabin at the Kishon. The victories near Megiddo — Gideon’s over Midian and Barak’s over Sisera — became proof that Jehovah could frustrate any enemy’s plans.

Additional Point

The name itself keeps the lesson alive: God’s final war is called Har–Magedon, “Mountain of Megiddo.” Every time God’s people hear that name, they are reminded that Jehovah’s ancient victories assure his certain triumph at Armageddon.

Psalm 83:9, 10

9Do to them as you did to Midian, As you did to Sisera and Jabin at the stream of Kishon. 10They were annihilated at En-dor; They became manure for the ground.

The Watchtower, October 15, 2008, pp. 14-15, ¶14 — “Jehovah’s Answer to a Heartfelt Prayer”

“Some four miles beyond the hill of Moreh, across the valley from Megiddo, lies Mount Tabor. There, Judge Barak had earlier gathered 10,000 Israelite troops to confront the army of Jabin, the Canaanite king of Hazor, under the command of his military chief Sisera. This Canaanite army had 900 war chariots equipped with deadly long blades of iron that turned with the wheels. As Israel’s poorly equipped troops assembled on Mount Tabor, Sisera’s army was lured into the valley. Then, ‘Jehovah began to throw Sisera and all his war chariots and all the camp into confusion.’ Likely, a sudden downpour of rain caused the chariots to get bogged down because of the overflowing Kishon River. The entire army was slaughtered by the Israelites.—Judg. 4:13-16; 5:19-21.”

The Watchtower, October 15, 2008, p. 15, ¶15

“The psalmist begs Jehovah to do something similar to the nations who threaten Israel’s existence in his day. He prays: ‘Do to them as to Midian, as to Sisera, as to Jabin at the torrent valley of Kishon. They were annihilated at En-dor; they became manure for the ground.’ (Ps. 83:9, 10) Significantly, God’s final war against Satan’s world is called Har–Magedon (meaning ‘Mountain of Megiddo’), or Armageddon. That name reminds us of the dramatic battles that took place near Megiddo. Jehovah’s victory in those ancient wars assures us of his certain triumph in the battle of Armageddon.—Rev. 16:13-16.”

Dig Deeper 4 · p. 84
4. What is noteworthy about the way Deborah praised Jael? (w15 8/1 15 ¶3) A
Main Point

Deborah did not begrudge Jael the praise. She and Barak were inspired to call Jael “most blessed of women” for her courageous deed, and Deborah cared only that Jehovah’s word had come true — that Sisera would fall by a woman’s hand.

Additional Point

That generosity of spirit is the point. Deborah could have claimed the victory for herself — she was the prophetess who sent Barak — but she sang Jael’s praise instead. When Jehovah’s word comes true, the faithful rejoice in it, whoever carried it out.

Reflect 1 · p. 85
As Jael needed courage, when might a sister with an unbelieving husband need courage?
Main Point

A sister may need Jael’s courage when her husband opposes her worship — when he forbids meetings, mocks her faith, or pressures her to compromise. Like Jael, she must think fast, stay loyal to Jehovah, and act with a mind of her own while keeping peace at home.

Additional Point

Jael’s husband was allied with Israel’s enemy, yet she sided with Jehovah anyway — and her husband was away when the test came. A sister today may face the same lonely choice, and Jehovah sees her courage just as he saw Jael’s.

Reflect 2 · p. 85
Jael was not a prophetess like Deborah, nor was she a soldier like Barak. Still, she did what she could. What do we learn from her about how to view any limitations we might have? B
Main Point

Jael was not a prophetess like Deborah, nor a soldier like Barak. She had no army, no sword, and no title — only a tent, a mallet, and a tent pin. Her limitations did not excuse her from acting; they were simply the tools Jehovah used.

Additional Point

We should view our limitations the same way: not as reasons to hold back, but as the shape of what we can do. Jael did what she could, with what she had, where she was — and Jehovah made her deed part of his victory song.

Reflect 3 · p. 85
In what other ways might you imitate Jael’s courage?
Main Point

We can imitate Jael by taking the initiative when Jehovah’s interests are at stake — speaking up for the truth, giving a witness, or helping a fellow Christian — even when we feel unqualified. She acted decisively, without waiting for someone else to do it.

Jael also imitated Rahab: she chose a side. Like Rahab, she sided with Jehovah against his enemies, though it cost her safety. We imitate her whenever we choose Jehovah’s side openly, even when the people around us have chosen the other one.

Bigger picture 1 · p. 85
What does this account teach me about Jehovah?
Main Point

Jehovah uses unlikely people to carry out his purposes. He gave the victory to a woman with a tent pin, not to an army, and he kept his word exactly: Sisera fell by a woman’s hand, just as Deborah foretold. No enemy is too strong for him.

Additional Point

Jehovah also watches. He saw Sisera flee, he saw Jael alone at the tents, and he had something in mind for the fugitive general. When we act with courage, we are never acting alone — Jehovah is watching, and his purposes are moving forward.
