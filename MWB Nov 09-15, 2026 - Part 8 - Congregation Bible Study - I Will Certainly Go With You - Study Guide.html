The setting — a dark time in Israel
Heart of it: Twenty years of terror, and Jehovah raises up a prophetess who sends for a deliverer.

Israel had rebelled against Jehovah, and he allowed their enemies to dominate them. Canaanite King Jabin was in power, and his general Sisera led a huge army with 900 chariots armed with iron weaponry. For twenty years Sisera’s army wreaked havoc: roads were deserted, villages empty, and few Israelite men had weapons or defenses of any kind. At that time Deborah, a brave woman, served as Jehovah’s prophetess and judged difficult cases the people brought before her. Jehovah used her to raise up Barak, a judge and saviour for his people, and she sent for him with Jehovah’s instructions: muster 10,000 men, take them to Mount Tabor, and Jehovah would give him victory over Sisera and his army. (pp. 78-79; summary of the chapter’s opening, not a quotation)

The account — Barak and Deborah face 900 chariots
Heart of it: A judge and a prophetess face 900 iron chariots, and the battle proves to be Jehovah’s.

Barak said he would go only if Deborah went with him — not from lack of faith, but because he knew Jehovah was using her and wanted as much of Jehovah’s guidance as possible. Deborah bravely agreed: “I will certainly go with you.” Barak mobilized 10,000 men and gathered them atop Mount Tabor, some 562 meters (1,844 ft) above the sweeping plain of Jezreel, where they could see Sisera’s chariots advancing. Rather than wait on the hillsides, Barak listened to Deborah and charged down onto the level plain. Jehovah threw the Canaanite army into confusion; rain fell in torrents, the valley became a marsh, and the chariots bogged down in the mud. Not one of Sisera’s soldiers remained alive. Sisera himself fled on foot — and Jehovah had something in mind for him. (pp. 78-79; summary of the chapter’s account, not a quotation)

For discussion · p. 79
In what ways did Barak and Deborah show courage?
Model answer

Deborah judged Israel, sent for Barak, and carried Jehovah’s orders to muster 10,000 men against 900 iron chariots. Barak obeyed, asking Deborah to go with him because he wanted Jehovah’s guidance close at hand. Together they left the safety of Mount Tabor, charged down onto the open plain, and trusted Jehovah to rout an army they could not defeat alone.

Advanced answer

Their courage was faith in action, not confidence in numbers. Barak had almost no weapons, yet he attacked on flat ground where chariots were strongest. Deborah risked her life beside the army, not behind it. Paul later named Barak among people of great faith, showing Jehovah remembered his courage long after the battle.

1. What was life like in Israel under Sisera’s domination? (w15 8/1 13 ¶1)
Model answer

For twenty years Sisera’s army terrorized Israel. Roads were deserted, villages emptied, and few men had weapons. We may imagine people afraid to farm, travel, or live in unwalled villages, fearing attack, capture, and rape. Canaanite rule was brutal, with child sacrifice and temple prostitution, and the very name Sisera caused terror and panic.

Advanced answer

The Watchtower notes that Deborah’s song shows travel was nearly impossible and village life had all but ended. We may imagine families cowering in woods and hills rather than risking the open roads. The oppression was not only military but moral: a brutal religion backed by 900 iron chariots, grinding a whole nation down for two decades.

Judges 5:6

In the days of Shamgar the son of Anath, In the days of Jael, the roads were deserted; Travelers kept to the back roads.

Judges 4:3

The Israelites cried out to Jehovah, because Jabin had 900 war chariots with iron scythes, and he harshly oppressed the Israelites for 20 years.

The Watchtower, August 1, 2015, p. 13, ¶1 — “I Arose as a Mother in Israel”

“… Sisera! The very name caused terror and panic in Israel. The Canaanite religion and culture were brutal, featuring child sacrifice and temple prostitution. What was it like to have a Canaanite general and his army dominating the land? Deborah’s song reveals that travel was nearly impossible in the land and village life had all but ended. (Judges 5:6, 7) We may imagine people cowering in the woods and hills, afraid to farm or to live in unwalled villages and terrified to travel on the open roads lest they be attacked, their children taken, and their women raped.b”

The footnote to that paragraph adds: “The song of Deborah indicates that Sisera often returned from his military actions with plunder that included girls, sometimes more than one for each soldier. (Judges 5:30) The word used for “girl” in that verse literally means “womb.” Such language reminds us that those women were valued mainly for their reproductive organs. Rape was likely commonplace.” (The Watchtower, August 1, 2015, p. 15, ftn. b)

The chapter’s own account agrees: “For 20 years, Sisera’s army wreaked havoc on the land. … As a result of this grim reign of terror, roads were deserted and the villages empty. Few Israelite men had weapons or defenses of any kind.” (Walk Courageously With God, p. 78)

2. Why is Deborah not usually counted among the 12 judges who served in Israel before Samuel became a prophet? (w86 6/1 31 ¶6-8)
Model answer

Twelve men served as judges between Joshua and Samuel: Othniel, Ehud, Shamgar, Barak, Gideon, Tola, Jair, Jephthah, Ibzan, Elon, Abdon, and Samson. Deborah is not counted with them because she was called first a prophetess, and she was linked with Judge Barak, who took the lead in delivering Israel.

Advanced answer

Deborah did judge cases — Israelites went up to her for judgment — but her role differed from the twelve. They were men especially “raised up” as saviours who led Israel to freedom through battle. Eli, likewise a judge in a sense, was principally a high priest, not a saviour-deliverer, so he is not listed either.

Judges 4:4

Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, was judging Israel at that time.

The Watchtower, June 1, 1986, p. 31, ¶6 — Questions From Readers

“When you count up the judges, the number you arrive at depends on how you view certain Israelites. But it can safely be said that 12 men served as judges between Joshua and Samuel.”

The Watchtower, June 1, 1986, p. 31, ¶7

“During the days of Moses and Joshua, some older men in the congregation were judges in the sense that they were chosen to hear and decide legal cases. (Exodus 18:21, 22; Joshua 8:33; 23:2) After Joshua’s death, Israel fell away from true worship and came to be afflicted by other peoples. Judges 2:16 says: “So Jehovah would raise up judges, and they would save them out of the hand of their pillagers.” Jehovah first raised up as a judge, or ‘savior,’ the man named Othniel. (Judges 3:9) After that came Ehud, Shamgar, Barak, Gideon, Tola, Jair, Jephthah, Ibzan, Elon, Abdon, and Samson.”

The Watchtower, June 1, 1986, p. 31, ¶8

“Aside from these 12, the Bible mentions Deborah, Eli, and Samuel in connection with judging. (Judges 4:4; 1 Samuel 4:16-18; 7:15, 16) However, Deborah is called first a prophetess, and she is linked with Judge Barak, who particularly took the lead in delivering the people from oppression. Similarly, Eli was principally a high priest, not a ‘savior’ who led Israel to freedom through battle. (Nehemiah 9:27) Hence, while Deborah and Eli had a role in judging Israel, there is reason not to list them with the 12 men who clearly and primarily were especially ‘raised up’ as judges. Acts 13:20 says that “judges [were given] until Samuel the prophet.” This limits what might be known as the period of the judges, and it shows why Samuel and his sons are usually not counted among the judges either. —1 Samuel 8:1.”

3. In what sense was Deborah “a mother in Israel”? (Judg. 4:4, 5; 5:7; w15 8/1 13 ¶2) A
Model answer

Deborah was a mother to Israel in a figurative sense. We do not know if she was a mother literally, but Jehovah assigned her to give the nation motherly protection. She rose up when village life had ended, summoned Barak, and directed him to deliver God’s people from Sisera.

Advanced answer

A mother protects, feeds, and gathers her children — and Deborah did that for a whole nation. She sat under her palm tree settling disputes, then took the lead in a war of deliverance. Her “motherhood” was not about status but about care: she acted until the villages lived again.

Judges 4:4, 5

4Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, was judging Israel at that time. 5She used to sit under Deborah’s palm tree between Ramah and Bethel in the mountainous region of Ephraim; the Israelites would go up to her for judgment.

Judges 5:7

The villagers in Israel were no more; They were no more until I, Deborah, rose up, Until I arose as a mother in Israel.

The Watchtower, August 1, 2015, p. 13, ¶2 — “I Arose as a Mother in Israel”

“Terror reigned for 20 years, until Jehovah saw evidence that his stubborn people were ready to change or, as the inspired record of the song of Deborah and Barak says, “Until I, Deborah, rose up, until I arose as a mother in Israel.” We do not know if Deborah, the wife of a man named Lappidoth, was a mother in a literal sense, but this expression was intended figuratively. In effect, Jehovah assigned Deborah to provide the nation with motherly protection. He commissioned her to summon a strong man of faith, Judge Barak, and direct him to rise up against Sisera. —Judges 4:3, 6, 7; 5:7.”

Dig Deeper 4 · p. 80
4. What did Deborah and Barak mean when they sang that “the stars fought” against Sisera? (Judg. 5:20; w05 1/15 25 ¶5)
Model answer

The song pictures the heavens fighting for Israel. Jehovah sent torrential rain that turned the Jezreel valley into a marsh, bogging down Sisera’s iron chariots. The “stars” are poetic language for divine intervention from heaven — Jehovah himself fighting, not literal stars leaving their orbits.

Advanced answer

The Watchtower of January 15, 2005, says the Bible does not say whether this involved angelic assistance, meteorite showers, or false astrological predictions for Sisera. What is certain is divine intervention: the storm, the flooded Kishon, and the confusion all came from Jehovah.

Judges 5:20

From heaven the stars fought; From their orbits they fought against Sisera.

Judges 5:21

The torrent of Kishon washed them away, The ancient torrent, the torrent of Kishon. You trampled down the powerful, O my soul.

The Watchtower, January 15, 2005, p. 25, ¶5 — “Highlights From the Book of Judges”

“5:20—How did the stars fight from the heavens in behalf of Barak? The Bible does not say whether this involved angelic assistance, meteorite showers that were interpreted ominously by Sisera’s wise men, or perhaps astrological predictions for Sisera that proved false. Undoubtedly, however, there was some type of divine intervention.”

The chapter’s own account gives the storm behind the poetry: “But Jehovah saw their courage, and he rewarded them for it. He threw the Canaanite army into confusion. Rain fell in torrents. Soon the valley of Jezreel was like a great marsh, and those fearsome chariots got bogged down in the mud.” (Walk Courageously With God, p. 79)

Reflect 1 · p. 81
When might a brother imitate the way Barak humbly accepted the help of a faithful woman? B
Model answer

A brother can imitate Barak by welcoming the help of a faithful sister instead of feeling threatened by it. That might mean accepting her experience, her suggestions, or her encouragement in the ministry, in caring for others, or in a congregation project — and giving Jehovah’s guidance through her its full weight.

Advanced answer

Barak did not ask Deborah along to share blame; he wanted as much of Jehovah’s guidance as possible. Humility is not weakness — Paul listed Barak among people of great faith. A brother shows the same humility when he values a sister’s faith and lets her spiritual strength help him serve better.

Why did Deborah need courage to accompany Barak? When do we need similar courage?
Model answer

Deborah had to leave her palm tree and go to war — to Mount Tabor, then down onto the plain against 900 iron chariots, with almost no weapons on her side. She risked her life beside the army. We need similar courage when standing for Jehovah puts us in the open, not on safe ground.

Advanced answer

Deborah was not a soldier; she was a prophetess and judge. Yet she went because Jehovah had spoken, and she stayed through the charge. We imitate her when we speak up for truth at work, at school, or with family, knowing opposition may follow.

Reflect 3 · p. 81
In what other ways might you imitate the courage of Barak and Deborah?
Model answer

We can obey Jehovah’s direction promptly, even when it looks unwise — Barak charged downhill onto flat ground. We can encourage others the way Deborah encouraged Barak, and we can trust Jehovah to fight for us when we are outnumbered, out-armed, and afraid.

Advanced answer

Their courage was not a single moment: Deborah judged faithfully before the battle, and Barak obeyed when the call came. Imitating them means steady courage — daily faithfulness, not only brave gestures when a crisis comes.

Bigger picture 1 · p. 81
What does this account teach me about Jehovah?
Model answer

Jehovah notices the oppressed and acts for them. He raised up a prophetess and a judge, gave victory to an outnumbered army, and turned Sisera’s greatest strength — his chariots — into a trap. He fights for those who trust him, and no human power can stand against him.

Advanced answer

Jehovah used unlikely people: a woman as prophetess and a reluctant-seeming general as deliverer. He also kept his word precisely — the victory came, and Sisera fell by a woman’s hand, just as Deborah foretold. Jehovah’s purposes never depend on human strength.

Judges 4:15

Judges 4:23

So on that day, God subdued Jabin the king of Canaan before the Israelites.

Jehovah is the actor throughout the chapter: he “allowed their enemies to dominate them” when Israel rebelled, then “used [Deborah] to raise up a judge and savior,” and at the battle “He threw the Canaanite army into confusion.” (Walk Courageously With God, pp. 78-79)

Bigger picture 2 · p. 81
How does the account tie in with Jehovah’s purposes?
Model answer

Jehovah kept his purpose for Israel alive. Though the people had rebelled, he raised up judges to save them and subdued King Jabin, preserving the nation in the land. The victory also pointed forward: the same God who delivered Israel then will bring complete deliverance through his Kingdom.

The book of Judges shows Jehovah responding again and again when his people called on him, even after they had turned away. As the January 15, 2005 Watchtower notes, these deeds of salvation foreshadow the greater deliverance to come through Jesus Christ — and our deliverance is part of that same purpose.
